What is DeFi?

Read the film transcript

You buy a coffee and tap your card. A few seconds later, your phone says you paid five dollars. Now imagine paying a friend five digital tokens. You might see a similar little check mark. But behind the screen, something different happened. Who kept the record? And who had permission to change it? Those two questions will get you most of the way to understanding DeFi.

With an ordinary bank payment, financial institutions update their records. With a blockchain payment, computers on a network check the transaction against shared rules and agree on the updated record. Your wallet shows you that record and lets you authorize actions. Think of it as the tool you use to give an instruction. The balance itself is recorded on the network.

Now give that network a program that can do more than record a payment. It can follow an exchange rule, for example. Programs like this are called smart contracts. DeFi stands for decentralized finance. It’s a name for financial services, like exchanges or loans, built using these programs. Let’s slow down and look at one exchange from the point of view of the person using it.

Alex offers ten blue tokens. The app estimates twenty orange tokens in return. Look at the second number on the screen. The minimum is nineteen orange. Before Alex confirms, the price moves. Now the exchange would deliver only eighteen. Pause for a moment. Would you expect that swap to go through? If the contract enforces Alex’s minimum, the answer is no. A submitted transaction can still cost a network fee.

Here’s the subtle part. Software following a rule doesn’t tell you whether it’s a good rule for you. The contract can contain a mistake. Someone may have permission to change it. And a wallet can faithfully carry out an instruction you misunderstood. That’s why learning to read the request matters more than learning where the confirm button is.

Think back to the coffee. For a bank payment, ask who keeps the account record. For a DeFi swap, add two more questions. What rule will the program apply? What permission am I giving it? If you can explain those answers in your own words, you’ve understood the core idea. In the next lesson, we’ll look at the wallet and the one piece of information nobody needs in order to send you money.

When you send money through a bank, the bank updates its records. A blockchain is a shared record that a network of computers maintains. Some blockchains also run small programs called smart contracts.

DeFi is short for decentralized finance. A DeFi app can use a smart contract to exchange tokens or manage a loan. You use the app to see the terms, and your wallet to authorize the action.

Alex wants to exchange 10 blue tokens for orange ones. Before agreeing, Alex can read what the exchange offers. The contract applies the rules, even if Alex misunderstood them.

What does a wallet do?

Read the film transcript

Sam wants to send Alex two practice tokens. He asks for the receiving address. Alex opens a wallet, sees a long string of letters and numbers, and hesitates. Is that string safe to share? Let's follow the payment from the beginning.

A blockchain keeps a record of balances and transactions. A wallet is the tool Alex uses to read that record and authorize changes. Think about a banking app for a moment. Deleting the app doesn't erase the bank's records. In a similar way, deleting a wallet app doesn't erase the blockchain. What can be lost is the ability to authorize the next transaction.

For this example Alex uses a self-custody wallet. A private key authorizes actions. A recovery phrase can restore the keys for many wallets. Someone who obtains those words may gain control of the assets. A public receiving address has a different job. It gives Sam a destination. He never needs Alex's recovery words to send anything.

Now look at the practice payment. The asset is two test tokens. The destination is Alex's practice address. The network should be Testnet A. Sam's screen shows Testnet B. The address looks exactly right. Would you ask Sam to press send? Take a moment to decide. Stop here. An address can look valid on two networks, while each network keeps its own balances. Match the asset, address and network independently before proceeding.

Suppose Sam corrects the network and the practice transfer succeeds. A transaction identifier, sometimes called a hash, can be used to look up the recorded result on the matching network explorer. A screenshot alone tells Alex less than that record. In this course, imaginary examples are enough. A real wallet or payment is optional.

One last distinction. Connecting a wallet lets a website see information such as a public address. A later request to sign or approve spending is a separate decision. Read what it authorizes. If someone says they need your secret words to verify an incoming payment, close the request. The short version is simple. Share a receiving address when needed. Protect recovery information. Check the network every time.

Your balance lives on the network. A wallet lets you view it and authorize actions. A public address tells someone where to send tokens. With a self-custody wallet, the recovery phrase can restore control over your accounts. Keep it private.

A network is the system recording the transaction. Before sending, match three things with the recipient: the asset, the receiving address and the network.

Sam asks for two test tokens on Network A. The wallet shows Network B. Even if the address looks right, Alex stops and checks the network. You can finish this course without creating a wallet.
03 / 06

What am I holding?

Read the film transcript

Imagine opening a wallet and seeing ten tokens beside a dollar sign. Does that mean you can sell them for exactly ten dollars? Before we calculate anything, ask a simpler question. What do those ten units actually represent?

A token is a unit recorded on a network. Some tokens give access to a service. Some are used in voting. Others try to track the price of something familiar. The name and logo tell you very little about the rights that come with holding one. Two unrelated tokens can even use the same name.

Take a dollar-targeting stablecoin. Its design aims to keep one token close to one dollar. Suppose Alex holds ten units. At a market price of one dollar each, the total is ten dollars. Now the screen shows a market price of ninety cents. Alex still owns ten tokens. How much are they worth before fees? Pause and do the multiplication. Ten times ninety cents is nine dollars. Token count and market value answer two different questions.

Why might the price move? Some designs rely on assets held in reserve. Others use crypto collateral and rules for selling it if prices fall. Some rely on incentives that people may stop following. To understand one particular token, ask who supports the target price, what evidence is available, who can redeem it, and on what terms. Selling on a market and redeeming from an issuer can produce different outcomes.

Now consider a token described as linked to a bond. The token transfer may be recorded on a blockchain. The bond, its custodian, and the holder's legal claim can be outside that record. A useful question is what document explains who receives payments and what happens if the issuer cannot deliver. The word backed does not settle those questions by itself.

Here is a quick habit to keep. Before asking what a token might be worth tomorrow, describe what one unit gives its holder today. Identify the network and official token address. Find the rules for redemption or exit. If you can't explain the claim yet, you've found exactly what you need to research.

A token is a unit recorded on a network. Different tokens give holders different rights. A stablecoin aims to follow a reference price, often one US dollar. Its price can drift away from that target.

A real-world asset token may represent a claim linked to something outside the blockchain, such as a bond. The issuer’s terms explain the holder’s rights and whether the token can be redeemed.

Alex holds 10 dollar-targeting stablecoins. At $1 each the balance is worth $10. If each trades for $0.90, the same 10 tokens are worth $9 before fees.

What happens in a swap?

Read the film transcript

Alex has ten blue tokens and wants orange ones. The exchange screen says twenty orange. That number looks simple enough. But before Alex agrees, let's read the other lines on the screen. Each answers a different question.

Start with the pair. Which token will leave the wallet and which will arrive? A name can be copied. Check that both token identities and the network match the intended trade. Many decentralized exchanges draw from pools of assets supplied by users. When someone adds blue and takes out orange, the mix in the pool changes. The pricing rule and the size of the trade affect the quote.

Next read the estimate. Twenty orange is the app's estimate at this moment. The minimum received is nineteen. That number sets a condition on execution. If the contract enforces nineteen and only eighteen are available when the trade executes, the swap fails. If nineteen and a half are available, the minimum condition is satisfied. Other transaction checks can still matter.

What about the fee? A network processes the submitted transaction, and processing can cost a fee often called gas. The exchange may also charge a trading fee. Look for whether the displayed output already includes that fee. A failed transaction can still consume network gas. Cancelling a request in your wallet before signing is a different situation from a submitted transaction that later fails.

Finally, check the spending permission. A site may request permission for a contract to use some of a token balance. If Alex intends to exchange ten tokens and the screen asks for unlimited spending, that is a much broader permission. Disconnecting the site later does not usually cancel an existing on-chain allowance. An unclear permission is a good reason to stop.

Try reading the quote aloud. I give ten blue. I expect about twenty orange. My minimum is nineteen. I know the network fee and which contract can spend my tokens. If any part of that sentence is missing, return to the quote. Knowing what you are authorizing is the skill this lesson is designed to teach.

A swap exchanges one token for another. Many decentralized exchanges trade against a pool of tokens supplied by other users. The app quotes what you might receive. A network fee, often called gas, pays for processing.

Check the token, the minimum amount you will receive, the fee and the spending permission. Connecting a wallet lets a site see your public address. A separate approval may let a contract spend tokens.

Alex offers 10 blue tokens. The estimate is 20 orange, with a minimum of 19. If the contract can deliver only 18, a swap enforcing that minimum fails. A submitted transaction can still cost gas.

Where do rewards come from?

Read the film transcript

A dashboard promises rewards. Alex sees a percentage in large print. Before asking how much, ask who pays and why. Three activities can display similar numbers while involving very different work and risk.

First, lending. Imagine a borrower pledges collateral and borrows another asset. The borrower may pay interest. Part of that interest can go to suppliers under the protocol's rules. A supplier still faces risks, including whether enough assets are available to withdraw. If the borrower's collateral falls too far, liquidation rules may allow it to be sold or transferred to repay debt.

Second, liquidity. Traders need assets to exchange. A liquidity provider places assets into a pool and may receive a share of trading fees. Over time the pool can contain a different mix of tokens. Even after collecting fees, the provider may end up with less value than simply holding the original tokens. The trading fee is revenue. It tells you little on its own about the final result.

Third, network staking. In a proof-of-stake network, validators commit assets to help check and propose blocks. Rewards and penalties follow that network's rules. Using a staking provider adds another party and may involve withdrawal delays. A product using the word staking on its website may have a different mechanism, so look for the work that actually earns the reward.

Let's put a price on a reward. Alex earns two reward tokens. At a dollar each, the two are worth two dollars. A week later each trades for twenty-five cents. What are the two reward tokens worth now? Pause here. Together, they're worth fifty cents before costs. The count stayed the same while the dollar value changed. For the whole position, you would also need to value the original assets and include fees.

When you see a quoted annual rate, ask whether it assumes reinvestment, whether the rate can change and in which asset rewards are paid. Then finish this sentence in your own words. The reward comes from this activity, and here is how my total position could lose value.

Ask who pays and why. In lending, borrowers may pay interest. In a liquidity pool, traders may pay fees to the people who supplied the tokens. In network staking, participants commit assets to help validate the network and may receive rewards.

Each activity has a different way to lose money. Borrowers can lose collateral when its value falls. Pool assets and staking rewards can fall in price. A displayed rate can change.

Alex earns two reward tokens worth $1 each. Later they trade for $0.25 each. The reward is now worth $0.50. Counting tokens alone misses the change in value.

When should I stop?

Read the film transcript

Alex receives a message that looks like it came from support. It says a refund is waiting and asks for one quick wallet signature. The logo looks familiar and no one has asked for a password. Would you sign? Let's take the pressure out of that decision.

First, look at how the request arrived. Alex did not start a support conversation. The link came in a direct message, and the sender says the offer will expire. A familiar name can be copied and urgency discourages checking. Alex closes the message and independently opens a known official support channel. A link supplied by the stranger cannot independently verify the stranger.

Now look at what the wallet is being asked to do. Receiving a simple payment normally requires a public receiving address and the correct network. It doesn't require giving a stranger your recovery words. A signature can also matter even when the words never leave your device. Depending on the request, it can authorize spending or other rights. If you cannot read or explain the permission, decline it.

A legitimate-looking project deserves careful questions too. An audit badge says little by itself. Which version of the code did the reviewers examine? When? Were the issues fixed? Can someone upgrade the contract after the review? A large displayed balance or a high reward rate doesn't answer whether you can withdraw. Good research often ends with a written list of things you still don't know.

Let's return to the refund. Alex closes the link, goes to support independently and finds no matching refund notice. What should Alex do with the original signature request? Leave it unsigned. There is no need to experiment with real funds to learn that lesson.

You have reached the end of the DeFi overview. Before using any unfamiliar app, ask four questions. What do I hold? Who controls it? How do I exit? What could go wrong? If the answer to one is missing, stopping is a valid decision. That's a practical skill you can carry into every case study in the Academy.

Pause when a message pressures you, a site asks for recovery words, or a wallet request grants permissions you cannot explain. Open the official site independently. A logo, a high return or an audit badge alone cannot establish safety.
