DEFI4U INCOME ACADEMY · MODULE 1

DeFi in one sitting

Six short reads and three questions. Allow about 10–15 minutes. All examples use imaginary balances.

01 / 06

What is DeFi?

When you send money through a bank, the bank updates its records. A blockchain is a shared record that a network of computers maintains. Some blockchains also run small programs called smart contracts.

DeFi is short for decentralized finance. A DeFi app can use a smart contract to exchange tokens or manage a loan. You use the app to see the terms, and your wallet to authorize the action.

Alex wants to exchange 10 blue tokens for orange ones. Before agreeing, Alex can read what the exchange offers. The contract applies the rules, even if Alex misunderstood them.
02 / 06

What does a wallet do?

Your balance lives on the network. A wallet lets you view it and authorize actions. A public address tells someone where to send tokens. With a self-custody wallet, the recovery phrase can restore control over your accounts. Keep it private.

A network is the system recording the transaction. Before sending, match three things with the recipient: the asset, the receiving address and the network.

Sam asks for two test tokens on Network A. The wallet shows Network B. Even if the address looks right, Alex stops and checks the network. You can finish this course without creating a wallet.
03 / 06

What am I holding?

A token is a unit recorded on a network. Different tokens give holders different rights. A stablecoin aims to follow a reference price, often one US dollar. Its price can drift away from that target.

A real-world asset token may represent a claim linked to something outside the blockchain, such as a bond. The issuer’s terms explain the holder’s rights and whether the token can be redeemed.

Alex holds 10 dollar-targeting stablecoins. At $1 each the balance is worth $10. If each trades for $0.90, the same 10 tokens are worth $9 before fees.
04 / 06

What happens in a swap?

A swap exchanges one token for another. Many decentralized exchanges trade against a pool of tokens supplied by other users. The app quotes what you might receive. A network fee, often called gas, pays for processing.

Check the token, the minimum amount you will receive, the fee and the spending permission. Connecting a wallet lets a site see your public address. A separate approval may let a contract spend tokens.

Alex offers 10 blue tokens. The estimate is 20 orange, with a minimum of 19. If the contract can deliver only 18, a swap enforcing that minimum fails. A submitted transaction can still cost gas.
05 / 06

Where do rewards come from?

Ask who pays and why. In lending, borrowers may pay interest. In a liquidity pool, traders may pay fees to the people who supplied the tokens. In network staking, participants commit assets to help validate the network and may receive rewards.

Each activity has a different way to lose money. Borrowers can lose collateral when its value falls. Pool assets and staking rewards can fall in price. A displayed rate can change.

Alex earns two reward tokens worth $1 each. Later they trade for $0.25 each. The reward is now worth $0.50. Counting tokens alone misses the change in value.
06 / 06

When should I stop?

Pause when a message pressures you, a site asks for recovery words, or a wallet request grants permissions you cannot explain. Open the official site independently. A logo, a high return or an audit badge alone cannot establish safety.

Before trying any product, ask four questions. What do I hold? Who controls it? How do I exit? What could go wrong? If a key answer is missing, you can stop there.

A stranger offers Alex a refund and asks for a signature. Alex closes the message and checks with support through a known official channel. A harmful request can ask for a signature without ever asking for recovery words.
